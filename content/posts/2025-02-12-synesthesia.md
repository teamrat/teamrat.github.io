About fifteen years ago, I was watching a PBS documentary. I don't remember the title or most of the details, but there was a segment on synesthesia—the neurological phenomenon where senses cross-wire. Some people see colors when they hear music. Others taste words.

I remember sitting there thinking: "Huh!"

Not because the concept was strange. But because I suddenly realized that the way I experience numbers and time might not be universal.

I thought everyone had a mental image of numbers.

Mine is three-dimensional and contextual. For a generic number or set of numbers, I'm always at zero. Negative numbers extend behind me—down and to the left. Positive numbers go forward and up to my right. The line bends slightly at certain points: left at 10, left again at 20, right at 50, left at 100, right at 1,000. After 10,000, it starts to fade into the distance. The stretch between 50 and 100 falls right in front of me, pointing up like an uphill road. The whole system rotates with my head.

When the numbers represent time, it's completely different. Hours of the day, days of the week, days in a year—all laid out on circles.

I am always at the present moment.

Hours of the day form a flat circle that I see from the side—like looking at a clock edge-on. Days of the week sit slightly below that, at an angle I have to look down to see. Months and dates within a year form an oval at eye level. All the circles move counter-clockwise, with a slight tilt up to the right.

Specific dates on the calendar follow a different line—not the generic number line. The past extends behind me, down and to the left. Days before I was born (1973) are out of focus, getting blurrier the farther back they go. I'm not sure if the line has a fixed shape. But the past is behind me and moves down and to the left. There's a clear bend at 1900, to the left. Beyond that, the line moves through dark, foggy space. I sometimes notice a bend there. Deep geologic time and BC are strange concepts—difficult to position.

The immediate past and future tangle with the yearly calendar circle. But a few years out, they blend back into it. The future points upward. There's a bend to the left at 2100, then it fades to mist.

When I need to remember something from the past or imagine something in the future, I have to move myself to that point on the timeline. It takes a moment to reorient. Sometimes I don't succeed. But when I do—when I'm remembering a memory or reliving an emotion—I'm not observing it from here. I'm **there**. At that point in time.

I recently discovered that moving my eyeballs while thinking of numbers makes it easier. It turns out repositioning myself works better when I move my eyeballs.

---

When I think of taking my daughter to piano at 5:15 and now it is 4:10, I see both points on the circle. I position myself at 4:10 and see where I have to be. I'm not calculating the gap—I'm measuring the arc between two visible points.

My wife hands me receipts at restaurants and asks me to calculate the tip. I'm slow. Painfully slow for someone who works with differential equations daily. It's become a running joke between us—I'll push the receipt back across the table with a sheepish look.

It's not that I can't do arithmetic. It's that I have to navigate to the numbers spatially. I see them positioned in three-dimensional space, and addition means moving between those positions. What takes others a glance takes me... effort. Justified or not, I use this as my excuse.

Upcoming trips work differently. When I think about one next week and another in two months, I zoom in and out on the timeline. The trip next week has a specific date, so it's not on the daily circle anymore. It's positioned somewhere else in the architecture—on the yearly oval, eye-level, tilted up to the right.

It's hard thinking about thinking. Even writing this, I have to imagine scenarios to activate the numbers in space. I can't observe the system in vacuum. The spatial positioning only reveals itself when I'm actually using it—calculating a tip, planning a trip, remembering when something happened.

The architecture isn't fixed. When I worked on nuclear waste disposal modeling, I needed to think in thousand-year timescales. The 1,000 to 10,000 year range—normally invisible, lost in the mist—came into focus. Distant points, but suddenly positionable. The spatial framework adapted to what I needed to see.

---

After that PBS documentary, I started having conversations with friends who study psychology and behavioral science. They gave me language for what I'd always experienced. This is called synesthesia—specifically, a form involving spatial visualization of numbers and time.

Not formally diagnosed. Just recognized.

I learned that most people don't have a mental number line. They don't see time in circles. They don't have to reposition themselves to access memories or imagine future events.

For them, numbers are... what? Symbols? Abstractions? I genuinely don't know. I only know what I see.

In college, I learned about complex numbers—numbers that exist on a plane rather than a line, with a real axis and an imaginary axis. The i-axis, orthogonal to everything I'd known.

I was surprised. The number line had to add a dimension I hadn't anticipated. For once, the spatial architecture I'd always relied on didn't naturally accommodate a new mathematical concept. The i-axis didn't have a home in my existing structure.

I rarely think in those terms now. Complex numbers remain more symbolic than spatial for me—an exception to the way I usually experience mathematics.

---

I don't know if this spatial framework plays into my mathematical work. I genuinely can't say whether it helps or hinders, because I don't know the alternative. This is how it has always been.

What I do know is this:

When I see numbers, they exist in space. When I think about time, I move through it. When I remember the past, I position myself there. When I imagine the future, I have to reorient.

I thought this is just how it is.

Now I know it's not.

But it's still mine.
