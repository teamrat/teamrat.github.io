---
title: "Independence Day"
date: 2025-05-24
tags: ["Eritrea", "politics"]
---

For most Eritreans of my generation, Independence Day[^1] is filled with vivid memories. I was a freshman in Addis Ababa, so my recollection of what transpired in Asmara comes from stories told by friends and family, and from videos and photos of liberation I watched countless times. As I pause to reflect today, I find myself thinking about one of the cameramen whose literal viewpoint of that historic day is burned into collective memory.

I met him briefly a couple of times—a low-key person with a bright smile. I remember him from a documentary that aired on EriTV, which he helped co-found, where he recounted the difficulty of suppressing his emotions so he could record history unfolding.

Years later, after he was let go from EriTV, he published a poignant photo of an Independence Day celebration at Asmara Stadium, taken from outside behind the closed metal bars of the stadium gates. If my memory serves me well, both the man and the camera that recorded the actual Independence Day were no longer welcome at the commemoration of that day.

A few years after that, the same week I started my academic career, he was arrested and remains behind another set of bars. As far as I know, there has been no publicly known indictment or sentencing.

He wasn't alone. Among the journalists who disappeared were two who had been in college at the same time as me—one in the same academic program. These weren't abstract casualties of authoritarian rule; they were people who had sat in the same lecture halls, shared the same youthful hopes for themselves and their country's future.

While that cameraman recorded the unfolding of the independence we were meant to celebrate, his life became the embodiment of freedom's absence. The photographer is just one of many whose chains were simply exchanged for different ones.

Some of us escaped unscathed.

The cost of this authoritarian consolidation extends far beyond individual stories of imprisonment. Eritrea today faces what can only be called a demographic paradox. Despite achieving the independence that was supposed to secure the nation's future, the country is hemorrhaging its most precious resource: its people. Particularly its young people, who flee in numbers that would be staggering if they weren't so tragically predictable.

When a government operates without accountability—when there is no independent legislature or judiciary to check executive power—people vote with their feet. The mass exodus from Eritrea represents a collective referendum on life under authoritarian rule. Families are torn apart not by war, but by the simple recognition that staying means accepting a future without fundamental freedoms.

This is what thirty-four years of unaccountable rule produces. Thirty-four years. The same government, the same leader, with no meaningful checks on power, no independent institutions, no space for dissent or democratic participation.

For years, I have avoided expressing political opinions, preferring the comfort of silence. But Martin Luther King Jr.'s words now feel like an accusation: "In the end, we will remember not the words of our enemies, but the silence of our friends." I feel the shame of that silence, the recognition that comfort and the desire to avoid disappointing others have kept me from speaking up for those who no longer can.

I owe an apology to journalist Seyoum Tsehaye, who captured our independence with such hope and now languishes in prison for his commitment to truth. I owe an apology to my college friends Amanuel Asrat and Medhane Haile, whose voices were silenced while I chose comfort over courage. 

Your sacrifice deserved better than my silence. This breaking of that silence, however late, is for you.

[^1]: This post is back dated to its original publication in an anonymous blog. It took me another year to attach my name to it.
